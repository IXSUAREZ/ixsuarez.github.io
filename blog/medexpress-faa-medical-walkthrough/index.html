MedXPress, Step by Step: Your FAA Medical Application Before the AME Visit

Diego Suarez
· August 4, 2026 · ~9 min read

Before your first FAA medical exam, there's a homework assignment most new student pilots don't know about: the application itself. It's an online form called MedXPress, and how you fill it out matters. A sloppy or panicked application is how people turn a routine $150 exam into a months-long deferral to Oklahoma City.

I walk students through this before their first Aviation Medical Examiner (AME) visit around Louisville. Two disclaimers up front, because they matter: I'm an instructor, not a doctor, and nothing here is medical or legal advice. If a question on the form genuinely applies to you, the right resources are your AME before your appointment and AOPA's medical services (if you're a member). The goal of this article is narrower: show you what the form is, what to gather, and how to avoid the unforced errors.

What MedXPress is — and why you do it before the visit

MedXPress (medxpress.faa.gov) is the FAA's online version of Form 8500-8, the application for an airman medical certificate. You fill it out at home, submit it electronically, and get a confirmation number. You bring that number to your AME appointment, the AME pulls up your application, reviews it with you, conducts the physical exam, and — if all goes well — prints your medical certificate on the spot.

The key timing rule: once you submit, your application is valid for 60 days. Don't fill it out three months before your appointment. But do fill it out a few days ahead, at a desk, unhurried — not in the AME's parking lot on your phone.

What to gather before you start

Half the battle is having your information in front of you so you're not guessing:

  • Your government ID (driver's license or passport) for the identity fields.
  • A list of every medication you currently take — name, dosage, and the condition it's for. Include over-the-counter stuff you take regularly.
  • Your history of doctor visits over the last three years — every visit to a physician, physician assistant, nurse practitioner, psychologist, or counselor, with the date, reason, and provider. Routine physicals count. Urgent care for a sprained ankle counts.
  • Dates and details of any surgeries, hospitalizations, or significant diagnoses in your lifetime.
  • Your total flight time, if you have any (student pilots: zero is fine).
  • Your FAA Tracking Number, if you already have an IACRA account. Not required for MedXPress, but handy.

If listing three years of medical visits sounds tedious — it is. Do it anyway, from your patient portal records, not from memory. The questions that follow are why.

Creating the account

At medxpress.faa.gov you create an account with an email and password, then start a new application. The system will log you out aggressively if you idle, so save as you go. Nothing is sent to the FAA until you deliberately click submit at the end — you can start, stop, and review as many times as you need.

The form, section by section

The early sections are administrative — name, address, contact info, which class of medical you're applying for. Student pilots going for a Private certificate apply for Third Class. (If you're not sure whether you need Third Class, or whether BasicMed or the Sport Pilot driver's-license path fits you better, read my BasicMed vs. Third-Class comparison first — it's worth deciding before you book the AME.)

Then come the questions people stumble on:

Visits to health professionals in the last 3 years. This is where your gathered list goes in. The most common first-timer mistake is under-reporting — forgetting the urgent care visit, the dermatologist, the counselor you saw for six sessions in college. The FAA cross-checks; an omission discovered later is a much bigger problem than the visit itself ever was.

Current medications. List everything you actually take. Many common medications are perfectly fine with the FAA. The question isn't "will they ground me for this pill" — it's whether the AME can see a complete, consistent picture.

The medical history yes/no block. A long list of conditions — vision trouble, heart issues, mental health, seizures, substance history, and more. Here's the thing to internalize: many "yes" answers are survivable and common. A history of a resolved condition, a childhood surgery, counseling years ago — these show up on thousands of applications, and many end with a certificate issued, sometimes after documentation. What turns a "yes" into a nightmare is hiding it. Falsifying a federal form is not a paperwork slip; it can end your aviation prospects entirely.

The honesty principle. When in doubt, disclose and let the AME sort it out. The FAA's system is set up to evaluate disclosed conditions. It has no mechanism to be understanding about discovered ones.

Submit, and send the confirmation number to your AME

After you review and submit, MedXPress gives you a confirmation number. Write it down or save the email, and give it to your AME's office when you book the appointment (many Louisville-area AMEs ask for it in advance). Bring your ID and your confirmation number to the visit. If you wear glasses or contacts, bring them. Bring documentation for anything in your history you flagged "yes" — a letter from your treating doctor about a resolved condition can turn a deferral into an issuance.

What happens at the AME visit

The exam itself is less dramatic than people fear. For a Third Class medical, expect:

  • Review of your MedXPress application, question by question, with a chance to explain anything.
  • Vision check (distant and near; color vision).
  • Hearing check (conversational voice test is common).
  • Blood pressure and pulse.
  • Urine test (screening for things like sugar and protein — it's not a drug test).
  • A general physical: heart, lungs, abdomen, extremities, basic neurological checks.

For a healthy young applicant the whole visit often runs 30–45 minutes, and the AME prints the certificate before you leave. Fees vary by examiner — in the Louisville area, budget roughly $100–$200.

If you get deferred: what that actually means

Sometimes the AME can't issue on the spot and must defer the decision to the FAA's Aerospace Medical Certification Division in Oklahoma City. Deferral is not denial. It means "the FAA's doctors need to review this, with more documentation." Deferrals commonly resolve with a letter asking for specific records or tests, and many end in issuance — sometimes a special issuance with conditions. The cost is time: months, sometimes. This is why I tell students to do the medical early in training, before they've spent serious money on airplane time, and why complicated histories are worth discussing with an AME informally before the official application.

Timing it into your training

The right sequence for most Louisville students: decide which medical path fits (Third Class vs. BasicMed vs. Sport Pilot), do MedXPress carefully, get the medical in hand, then invest heavily in flight hours. The medical is a few hundred dollars and an afternoon — it's the cheapest go/no-go checkpoint in the whole process. Once it's out of the way, the next steps are ground study and a realistic training plan, which is where I can actually help.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long is a MedXPress application valid after I submit?
60 days. Fill it out a few days before your AME appointment — not the same morning in a rush, and not months ahead.
What class of medical should a student pilot apply for?
Third Class covers Private Pilot privileges and is the standard choice for new students. But if the Sport Pilot path or BasicMed fits your goals, you may not need an FAA medical exam at all — decide before you book the AME.
Will a "yes" answer on the medical history disqualify me?
Usually not automatically. Many "yes" answers lead to documentation requests or a deferral rather than a denial, and many of those still end in issuance. What creates real trouble is a "no" that should have been "yes" — falsification is a federal issue and far worse than the condition itself.
How much does the FAA medical exam cost in Louisville?
Fees vary by examiner; roughly $100–$200 is a realistic local range for a Third Class exam, sometimes more for first-time applicants. Confirm the fee when you book.
What happens if my application gets deferred?
The AME forwards your file to the FAA's Aerospace Medical Certification Division for review. Expect requests for medical records or additional testing, and expect the process to take months. Deferral is not denial — many deferred applicants are eventually issued, sometimes with a special issuance. If you have a complicated history, talk to an AME informally before submitting the official application.
