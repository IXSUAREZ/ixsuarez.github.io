BasicMed vs. Third-Class Medical vs. Sport Pilot: Which One Do You Need?

Diego Suarez
· April 22, 2026 · ~9 min read

This is an educational article — not medical advice. If you have a specific condition that might affect your eligibility, talk to an AME or AOPA Medical Services before you do anything else.

Three medical pathways exist for a private pilot in the United States. They do different things, require different paperwork, and authorize different airplanes. Picking the wrong one will either waste your money or ground your flying, depending on which way you pick wrong.

The one-sentence summary of each

  • Third-Class Medical: The traditional FAA medical, issued by an Aviation Medical Examiner (AME). It is the usual medical qualification for exercising Private Pilot privileges and is one way to establish later BasicMed eligibility.
  • BasicMed: An alternative for pilots who've held an FAA medical (any class) at least once since July 2006. Lets you keep flying without renewing the traditional medical, subject to some airplane and passenger limits.
  • Sport Pilot medical qualification: A qualifying pilot may use a current and valid U.S. driver's license instead of an FAA medical certificate, subject to the limits in the regulations. Since the MOSAIC rule, Sport Pilot operations are not limited solely to the former light-sport-aircraft definition.

If you're starting from scratch, the question is simpler than it looks: what airplane do you eventually want to fly, and with how many passengers? The answer points to one of the three.

Third-Class Medical — the traditional path

What it authorizes:

  • Student pilot solo flight
  • Private Pilot, Recreational Pilot privileges
  • Sport Pilot privileges (you can, but most Sport Pilots skip this path on purpose)
  • Can be the basis for upgrading to BasicMed later

How you get it:

  1. Fill out MedXPress online (FAA Form 8500-8). This is the history-and-background form the AME will receive before your visit.
  2. Schedule with an Aviation Medical Examiner (AME). Several practice in the Louisville area, including at Bowman Field.
  3. Complete the AME exam. Usually about 30–45 minutes. The AME issues your medical certificate on the spot if you qualify straightforwardly.

Cost: $100–$200 for the exam, varies by AME.

Validity:

  • Under 40 years old: valid for 5 years for Private Pilot, Recreational, and Sport Pilot privileges.
  • 40 and over: valid for 2 years for most privileges.

Biggest pitfall: Starting training without confirming eligibility. If you have a history of a condition the FAA reviews carefully (ADHD, depression with medication, sleep apnea, certain cardiac history), your medical can be deferred to the FAA for a formal determination. That's not a denial, but it can take months. If there's any chance a deferral applies to you, get the medical process started before you start flight training.

BasicMed — the practical path for longtime pilots

BasicMed is an alternative medical qualification, not a first-medical shortcut. To use it, a pilot must have held a medical certificate that was valid at some point after July 14, 2006, meet the other eligibility conditions, and ensure the most recent medical was not denied, revoked, or suspended. See the FAA’s current BasicMed summary.

What it authorizes:

  • Private Pilot privileges in aircraft up to certain limits
  • VFR or IFR flight
  • Up to 6 passengers, in an aircraft authorized for no more than 7 occupants
  • Altitude at or below 18,000 feet MSL
  • Speed at or below 250 knots indicated
  • Not for compensation or hire, subject to the specific flight-instruction exception in 14 CFR §61.113(i)
  • Aircraft: maximum certificated takeoff weight 12,500 pounds (excluding transport-category helicopters)

How you get and maintain it:

  1. Take the BasicMed online medical course (free, from AOPA or Mayo Clinic). About 2 hours. Renews every 24 calendar months.
  2. Visit any state-licensed physician — not necessarily an AME — with the FAA Comprehensive Medical Examination Checklist (CMEC). Every 48 months.
  3. The physician reviews your history, does a basic exam, and signs the checklist. No form goes to the FAA unless there's a problem.
  4. Keep the signed checklist and course-completion certificate in your logbook.

Cost: The online course is free. A physician visit runs whatever your doctor charges for an office visit — typically $100–$250 without insurance.

Biggest pitfalls:

  • Some pilots confuse "my last FAA medical was in 2004" with "I qualify." It must be since July 2006.
  • BasicMed is valid in the United States and may be used in another country only when that country authorizes it. Check the destination country’s current rules before an international flight.

Sport Pilot — the simplest path

A Sport Pilot may use a current and valid U.S. driver’s license instead of an FAA medical certificate when eligible. A prior FAA medical denial, revocation, or suspension can affect that option; consult the current FAA rules before relying on it.

What it authorizes:

  • Fly an aircraft that meets the current Sport Pilot performance and design requirements
  • 1 passenger max
  • Night operations only after the required training and instructor endorsements, and with FAA medical or BasicMed qualification
  • VFR only (no instrument flight)
  • Additional altitude, equipment, and endorsement limits apply

Aircraft eligibility: MOSAIC replaced the old Sport Pilot tie to the Part 1 light-sport-aircraft definition with the standards in 14 CFR §61.316. The exact make, model, configuration, and required endorsements matter; do not use an old 1,320-pound/120-knot checklist to decide eligibility. The FAA’s Sport Pilot page explains the changes that took effect October 22, 2025.

Biggest pitfalls:

  • A denial on your most recent medical application, or a revoked or suspended most recent medical certificate, affects eligibility to use a driver’s license. Resolve eligibility with the FAA before flying.
  • Private Pilot airplane privileges require the applicable medical qualification, such as an FAA medical certificate or BasicMed when eligible.
  • Instrument operations are not Sport Pilot privileges. Night operations require the specific MOSAIC training, endorsements, and medical qualification described by the FAA.

Decision tree: which one applies to you?

Answer in order:

1. Do you plan to fly in IMC/IFR or carry more than one passenger?

  • Yes → Plan for Private Pilot privileges and the appropriate medical qualification; BasicMed is an option only if you meet its eligibility and operating limits.
  • No → continue.

2. Does your planned aircraft and operation fit the current Sport Pilot rules, including the aircraft’s §61.316 eligibility and any required endorsements?

  • Yes → Sport Pilot may be an option; verify the exact aircraft and qualification with an instructor.
  • No → Third-Class Medical.

3. Have you ever had an FAA medical (any class) since July 2006, and are you now looking to avoid renewing it?

  • Yes → BasicMed is your lowest-paperwork option.
  • No → Third-Class Medical first, then BasicMed becomes available later.

For most students starting from scratch who want the flexibility to eventually pursue Private Pilot privileges: Third-Class Medical is the right starting point. It preserves every option.

Finding an AME in Louisville

Several AMEs operate in the Louisville metro, including at Bowman Field. The FAA's AME Finder tool (faa.gov/pilots/amelocator) lists active AMEs by ZIP code — that's the most current, official list.

Before you schedule, ask the AME's office three questions:

  1. Do you see first-time FAA medicals routinely? (Some AMEs focus on renewals; a first-timer with any history is worth scheduling with someone experienced.)
  2. What's the cost? (This varies by AME; $100–$200 is a typical range in this area.)
  3. How do I submit MedXPress ahead of time? (Any AME will tell you how, but it's easier to ask now than on exam day.)

If you have a condition on "the list"

Certain conditions trigger mandatory review by the FAA's Aerospace Medical Certification Division. Most resolve — the FAA certifies pilots with managed diabetes, treated depression, cardiac history, sleep apnea, ADHD medication history, and many other conditions — but the paperwork path is slower.

Don't start this process alone. Two resources:

  • AOPA Medical Services (aopa.org/medical) — the gold-standard resource for specific medical questions.
  • Your AME — many AMEs specialize in complex medical histories. Ask before booking.

And don't lie on MedXPress. The FAA cross-references prescription records and VA records. Discovered omissions can result in revocation of your certificate years later. "Yes" answers that require follow-up are better than "no" answers that come back.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I fly a Cessna 172 on Sport Pilot medical?
Do not decide this from the former Light-Sport Aircraft definition. Since MOSAIC, Sport Pilot eligibility depends on the current §61.316 criteria and any required endorsements. Confirm the exact model and configuration with an instructor before planning a flight.
Does Sport Pilot count toward Private Pilot hours later?
Yes. Solo and dual hours logged as a Sport Pilot student or Sport Pilot count toward the Private Pilot 40-hour minimum.
If I have BasicMed, can I rent aircraft from flight schools?
Generally yes, if the aircraft is within current BasicMed limits (up to 12,500 pounds and seven authorized occupants, subject to the rule’s conditions) and the school accepts BasicMed for rentals. Policies vary by school.
Is BasicMed recognized in Canada or Mexico?
BasicMed can be used outside the United States only where the destination country authorizes it. Check the destination’s current rules before flight.
What if my AME defers me?
Deferral means the FAA — not the AME — makes the call. It's not denial. The FAA may request additional records or tests. Timing depends on the case; work with your AME on the next steps.
